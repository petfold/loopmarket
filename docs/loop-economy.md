# The Loop Economy: A Universal Combinatorial Marketplace

*An economy built from one kind of promise — and the loops that bring it to life*

## The core idea

Every economic intention in the world — I will drive to Lyon on Thursday, I have a spare basement, I can teach Bayesian statistics, I need someone to water my plants in August, I will sell you a kilogram of flour — can be expressed in exactly one form: a signed, content-addressed **offer**. "Content-addressed" means the offer is named by a fingerprint of its own text, so it can be stored anywhere, copied by anyone, and never silently altered; a decentralized storage network like Ethereum Swarm holds it without any company owning the marketplace.

What does an offer say? Just one thing, really: *here is what I give, and here is what I want for it.* But the "what" is described with unusual care, in a shared concept catalogue called the **OntoDAG** — a vast family tree of categories in which every entry knows what it is a kind of. A Braeburn is an apple, an apple is fruit, fruit is food; a courier run is transport, transport is a service. (The "DAG" part just means the tree allows an entry to have several parents: a tomato may sit under both "fruit" and "salad ingredient" without contradiction.) The quiet elegance of the design is that *time and place live in the same catalogue as things*. "Tuesday, 14:00–16:00" fits within "next week" exactly as "Braeburn" fits within "apple," and "within three kilometers of the station" fits within "the city." Meanings, minutes, and map regions are all ordered by one and the same relation — *fits-within* — and that is what lets a single matching engine handle all three at once. An offer, then, is one expression in this catalogue: the precise thing, the window in which it will be performed, the region where it applies, and how long the offer itself remains open.

And the price? Here the design makes a small, clever move. Every offer is priced on the offeror's own **personal scale** — a private measuring unit, meaningful to one person; an economist would call it a personal numeraire, and the record format calls it the maker's personal token, but it is bookkeeping, not money. If Amara offers piano lessons, dog-sitting, and her car on weekends, and wants groceries, bicycle repair, and euros, she does not need to publish nine separate exchange rates covering every pairing. She posts six offers, each priced on her one scale, and the ratios among them quietly encode her entire preference structure — and, usefully, cannot contradict each other, so nobody can arbitrage Amara against her own quotes. Nobody else ever needs to hold anything of Amara's or believe in her scale; the numbers exist only for the instant it takes a loop to pass through Amara and cancel out.

Now the word that gives the whole system its name. Picture every offer as an arrow: it points from the thing given toward the thing wanted. Lay millions of these arrows on a table and you have what mathematicians call a directed graph — a web of arrows, each with a definite direction. A **loop** is a path of arrows that returns to its own starting point: what Amara gives is what someone wants, what that someone gives is what a third person wants, and so on around until the last arrow points back at what Amara asked for. Three conditions make a loop real rather than wishful. Each handoff must be compatible in meaning — the catalogue must confirm that the offered thing genuinely *fits within* the wanted category, so that "weekly vegetable box" satisfies "fresh local produce" but "café shelf" does not satisfy "refrigerated storage." Each handoff must be compatible in space and time — the parcel really can be at the pickup point when the courier passes. And the loop must close at a profit: multiply the exchange ratios all the way around, and the product must come out above one, meaning everyone gets at least what they asked and there is surplus left over. (For those who like the machinery visible: take logarithms and "product above one" becomes "sum below zero," which turns profitable-loop-hunting into the textbook problem of finding negative cycles in a weighted graph — a problem route-finding algorithms have handled for seventy years.)

Who hunts the loops? Not the protocol itself. Loops are found by an open, competitive ecology of **solver agents** — independent programs, run by anyone, that ingest the public offer book and race one another to discover profitable cycles. A solver that finds one submits it to a settlement contract on a blockchain. The contract does not take anyone's word for anything: it re-checks that every handoff fits, that the timing works, that the ratios clear — and then commits all the legs *atomically*, an all-or-nothing stroke in which either every promise in the loop becomes binding at once or none do. From that instant, the ledger has decided; the physical world has days or weeks to catch up. Deposits locked alongside each promise, and small insurance bets layered on top of them, price the risk that it won't — we will meet both properly soon.

That is the whole system. Everything below is what happens when you turn it on.

## Nothing here is untested

Before the tour, a reassurance: almost every load-bearing part of this machine has already carried weight somewhere in the world. The novelty is the composition, not the components.

Trading in circles without national currency is not a thought experiment; it is a Swiss institution older than most banks' computer systems. In 1934, in the pit of the Depression, a group of businessmen founded what became the WIR — an economic circle in which member firms pay one another in a private unit, the WIR franc, that exists only inside the network. It grew from a few dozen founders to tens of thousands of small and medium businesses moving on the order of a billion francs' worth of trade a year, and economists who studied it found something lovely: WIR activity swells precisely when ordinary money gets tight, cushioning its members against recessions. A painter buys a laptop in WIR; the dealer buys flowers; the florist has her shopfront painted; the circle closes. Sardinia repeated the trick after 2008 — a network called Sardex signed up thousands of the island's businesses, clearing tens of millions of euros a year in mutual credit, with human brokers doing by hand exactly what our solvers would do by algorithm: noticing who could pay whom in kind. Community versions of the same idea — local exchange systems, time banks — have run in hundreds of towns for decades. The lesson from all of them is double-edged: the circles work, and they starve when the web of offers is too thin. Hold that thought; the cure is coming.

Finding the circles automatically is not speculative either. On public blockchains today, an exchange mechanism called CoW Protocol (the name puns on "coincidence of wants") collects trading intentions for about thirty seconds at a time, freezes them into a batch, and auctions the batch to competing solvers who hunt for rings of orders that can settle against each other directly, returning the discovered surplus to the traders. Independent solver firms have speciated and displaced one another in open competition, and the mechanism has settled tens of billions of dollars. It is the loop economy in miniature — restricted, so far, to purely digital tokens. The batch-auction idea itself has an even more respectable pedigree: financial economists (Budish, Cramton, and Shim, in a famous 2015 study) showed that trading in discrete batches rather than continuously converts a wasteful race to be *fastest* into an honest contest to offer the *best price* — after documenting that traders had spent hundreds of millions of dollars laying a straighter fiber-optic cable between Chicago and New York to save three milliseconds. We will want that lesson later, when our solvers start elbowing each other.

Matching people without prices at all is textbook economics — literally; the algorithms that assign students to schools and young doctors to hospital residencies earned their designers a Nobel prize. Prediction markets, whose odds we will borrow as risk gauges, have decades of track record aggregating dispersed knowledge into a single number. And the physical world has quietly built the loop economy's street furniture already: automated parcel lockers — steel cabinets opened by one-time codes, pioneered at scale in Poland and now studding street corners across Europe, sixty-odd thousand cabinets from a single operator — plus car-sharing fleets, bike couriers, and self-storage. None of this waits to be invented. It waits to be *joined*.

## Anatomy of a small loop

Begin with the simplest nontrivial case: a triangle, in a mid-sized town anywhere.

Amara offers two hours of piano teaching a week (March through June, within five kilometers of her flat) priced at 100 on her scale, and wants a weekly vegetable box, priced at 100 on the same scale. Bruno, who runs a market garden twelve kilometers out, prices vegetable boxes and bicycle repair on his own scale — his delivery bikes keep breaking. Chen fixes bicycles, and her daughter wants piano lessons.

No pair of them can trade. Amara doesn't fix bikes, Bruno doesn't want piano, Chen doesn't grow vegetables. In a world of two-way deals, these three intentions die unexecuted — the *double coincidence of wants*, the oldest failure mode in economics: for barter to work, I must want exactly what you have while you want exactly what I have, and that coincidence is rare. Money was invented largely to paper over it. The loop is the other cure.

A solver notices the triangle. It checks the catalogue: "weekly vegetable box" fits within Amara's wanted "fresh local produce, weekly." It checks the map: Bruno's delivery radius covers Amara's street. It checks the arithmetic: around the cycle, the ratios multiply out to a 4 percent surplus. It submits the loop to the settlement contract, which verifies everything independently and fires. Three commitments now exist, each secured by a **bond** — a deposit locked in the contract for the duration, like a builder's performance bond or a landlord's deposit in reverse, released automatically when the promised thing is verifiably done and forfeited if it isn't. Over the following weeks, lessons are taught, sprockets are trued, cabbages arrive, and the bonds quietly come home unclaimed. Most of the 4 percent surplus is rebated to Amara, Bruno, and Chen; a sliver pays the solver for noticing.

Two honest footnotes to this idyll. First, the split of that surplus, and the rules by which competing solvers' proposals are ranked, is an *auction design* question — and auction design is a deep, well-tilled field, full of sealed bids and clearing rules and clever incentive proofs, most of it never yet compiled into running code. Which auction exactly is a dial, not a law of nature; here we keep the simplest setting and note that a century of theory is available when the dial needs turning. Second, resist the slogan that "no money changed hands." No *national currency* appeared in this particular loop, true — but the system is emphatically not anti-money. Liquidity is a spectrum, and money is simply the most liquid thing on it; in a market this frictionless, plenty of people will price their offers directly in euros, francs, bitcoin, or privacy-preserving currencies, and plenty of loops will include a leg that is nothing but a currency conversion. People will also go on *thinking* in a familiar unit — anyone who has moved countries knows the months of mental arithmetic before the new prices feel like anything — so a stable unit of account remains a psychological necessity even when the plumbing underneath speaks only ratios. The loop economy doesn't abolish money; it demotes it from prerequisite to convenience.

## The gallery of loops

### Packet switching for atoms

The most transformative offer class is the humblest: *transport of an object up to size S and weight W, from region A to region B, in window T.* Millions of people traverse the world daily with empty boot space, empty pannier bags, empty seat-backs. Each such traversal is an offer waiting to be written down.

A parcel from a farmhouse in the Julian Alps to a flat in Vienna need not wait for a courier company to find the route profitable. A solver composes the route from parts: a neighbor drives to Ljubljana every Monday (leg one); a carpool driver does Ljubljana–Graz on Tuesdays (leg two); a train commuter carries small parcels Graz–Vienna daily (leg three); and between legs one and two sits a **storage offer** — a café near Ljubljana's station rents a shelf by the day. The catalogue vouches for every joint: the parcel (30×20×15 centimeters, two kilograms) fits within each leg's declared capacity, and "café shelf, indoor, unrefrigerated" satisfies "storage, dry, up to three days" — but would *not* satisfy it if the parcel's category were "perishable." The type system is the safety inspector.

Readers who know computer networks will recognize the shape: this is packet switching, for atoms. The parcel is a packet, the commuters are links, the café is a router's buffer, and the OntoDAG is the type system — the category system — that prevents a parcel from being routed down a link too small to carry it. And exactly as in packet networks, **aggregation offers** appear at the hubs: someone at the Ljubljana café posts "consolidation: up to eight small parcels into one sixty-liter crate," because one crate on the Graz carpool is cheaper than eight loose parcels, with a matching *disaggregation* offer at the far end to break the crate back apart. Container shipping — arguably the single greatest logistics invention of the twentieth century, the humble standardized box that cut ocean freight costs to a rounding error — re-emerges here spontaneously, at every scale at once, because the catalogue knows which boxes nest into which.

Nor is the cargo restricted to the inanimate. A seat is capacity like any other, and "transport of one passenger, door to door, wheelchair accessible" is an offer the catalogue types as readily as a parcel — ride-sharing rediscovered as one row of a more general table. And as we'll see further on, kilowatt-hours and gigabytes ride the same abstraction as cabbages. The word "physical" in this section's spirit marks not a limit but the *novelty*: information networks solved routing for bits long ago; what was missing was the type system careful enough to route matter.

### The economics of emptiness

An early draft of this essay flirted with *negative prices* — goods you pay people to take. Rubbish, rubble, waste oil. The instinct to include them is right; the arithmetic of doing it that way is wrong, and the repair is illuminating. The loop-finding trick — multiply ratios, or sum their logarithms — needs every rate to be a positive number; a minus sign in the middle of a cycle is sand in the gears (there is no logarithm of a negative number, and no sensible "ratio" either). But look closer and the minus sign was never real. Nobody sells rubble at minus twenty; they *buy a service* — "removal of one cubic meter of rubble" — at plus twenty. Equivalently, they are buying back something with honest positive value: the clean, empty, usable state of their own site. Emptiness is a good. The empty truck, the cleared barn, the vacant shelf — the gallery's recurring background character is exactly this: space in its valuable, unoccupied state.

Once disposal is priced as a positive service, the recycling economy becomes ordinary loop-fodder. A demolition firm buys "removal of clean brick rubble" at one corner of town; a reclamation builder buys "used brick, cleaned" at the other; a transport leg connects them, and a solver harvests the coordination failure — the demolition firm's disposal fee shrinks, the builder's material cost shrinks, and the gap between them pays the truck. Restaurants buy removal of used cooking oil; biodiesel cooperatives buy used cooking oil. Gardeners buy removal of green waste; a composting project buys green waste. In each pair, what one side calls a burden the other calls an input, and today the match fails only because no market is granular enough to see it. As the connections become routine, disposal fees drift toward zero and sometimes cross it — at which point the flow quietly relabels itself, and yesterday's waste is traded as today's commodity. The sign flip happens *between* market eras, in how we categorize the thing; it never has to happen inside a loop's arithmetic, which hums along on positive numbers throughout.

### Time-shifting and the basement warehouse

Storage offers turn every dry basement, garage corner, and barn into a micro-warehouse — and they turn storage itself into a strange kind of transport: moving an object not through space but *through time*. Seasonal loops follow. In September an orchard offers apples now against guaranteed cider in February; a farmhouse offers its cool cellar for the winter; a cidery offers pressing in January. A solver strings the loop in September, when nothing has yet happened: the entire cycle is a package of promises about the future, committed in one atomic stroke and performed across five months.

Here the insurance layer earns its keep, so let us finally look at it squarely. Each leg's bond we have met — the cellar owner's deposit sits locked, and if February arrives with the apples spoiled by a warm cellar, the deposit pays the loss. On top of the bonds sit the **nano-bets**: tiny prediction-market positions on whether each leg will perform. A prediction market is just a place where people stake small sums on a yes-or-no future event, and where the resulting price — forty cents for a ticket paying a dollar if "yes" — is readable as a probability, here 40 percent. The magic is not the gambling; it is that the odds are a continuously updated, skin-in-the-game estimate of risk, sharper than any rating agency. Through the winter, the odds on "the cellar stays cool" twitch with the weather and with the owner's visible diligence, and everyone in the loop can watch. Backup capacity is priced by the same market: a second cellar stands ready under a *partial* commitment — bonded at a tenth of the full amount, activated automatically if the primary leg's odds breach a trigger. A seven-leg loop is thus not a chain that snaps at its weakest link but a braid with spare strands — and the extra cost of the braid over the chain is itself set competitively, because backup is just another offer.

### Bridges, and the pensioner by the supermarket

The supermarket does not know it participates. A **bridge agent** — a program, at first run by the marketplace's own builders, later by anyone — reads the supermarket's online inventory and republishes it as offers: "one kilogram of flour, pickup at address X, during opening hours, valid twenty-four hours, priced 4.30 on the bridge's scale," alongside the reverse offer, "euros or stablecoins at a fixed rate against that same scale." The bridge is a market maker standing with one foot in the loop economy and one in the price economy, and its scale — pegged to ordinary money by its own standing offers — is an anchor point of liquidity: any loop that cannot close in kind can close through a bridge by turning one leg into ordinary money. Every webshop, every gig platform, every utility with an API becomes wrappable. The bridges are not a compatibility feature; they are the bootstrap and the invasion vector. The loop economy does not ask the old economy's permission — it ingests it, one adapter at a time, and every ingested service makes ten thousand new loops feasible. This is also the answer to the thinness that starved so many community currencies: bridges keep the graph dense from day one.

But a bridge need not be software, and here the design turns unexpectedly humane. Consider a pensioner whose flat overlooks a supermarket, and who has what the labor market no longer wants and the loop economy prizes: time, proximity, and reliability. She posts a standing offer — "fulfillment: I collect up to ten listed grocery items, pack them, and deposit the parcel in the locker bank on the corner, weekdays before noon." The locker bank — one of those code-operated steel cabinets already installed by the tens of thousands across Europe's street corners — is itself in the marketplace as a standing storage-and-handover offer: a compartment, dry and secure, holding up to forty liters for up to forty-eight hours, opened by whoever holds the one-time code, which the settlement contract releases to the next leg of the loop upon deposit. So the flour that a bridge agent sourced from the supermarket's inventory is picked by a neighbor, buffered in a steel box, and swept up by a commuter's evening drive — three offers by three parties who never meet, snapped together like rail segments. The pensioner is what one might call an *interface human*: a person serving as the adapter between a legacy institution and the network, paid in whatever her own offers ask for, euros included. There will be many of her. In a marketplace that can monetize slack, the people richest in slack — the retired, the students, the rural — are suddenly holding inventory.

### Teleportation by bookkeeping

A family relocating from Rotterdam to Porto posts one intention: *move our household, these dates, this budget.* What a good solver returns — months into its evolution, having learned the recurring shapes — is a single committed object composed of forty-one offers. A crew that packs a household into standard crates (an offer the catalogue types down to "piano, upright, stairway: two flights"). Three transport legs with two storage buffers. A cleaning service on the Rotterdam side whose payment leg is the proceeds of a bridge offer selling the surplus furniture. A return leg in which the truck — which would otherwise drive back empty — carries a Porto ceramicist's kiln north, and it is this leg, invisible to the family, that tips the whole loop into surplus: the solver's real product was noticing that two households' problems were each other's discounts.

And then the twist that shows how deep the catalogue's logic runs. For a fair fraction of the family's belongings, the solver's optimizer discovers that the cheapest transport is *no transport*. The one-year-old flat-pack kitchen chairs, standard model, good condition: hauling them 2,000 kilometers costs more than their value. But the catalogue knows the chairs at the level of their *class*, not their atoms — and somewhere near Porto, someone is selling the identical model, same vintage, same condition, while someone near Rotterdam wants exactly such chairs. So the loop sells the family's chairs to the Rotterdam buyer and buys the Porto seller's chairs for the family, and the two chair-objects swap owners without either moving a meter. Call it teleportation by bookkeeping: for any good that the ontology can certify as interchangeable within its class, transport competes against *re-matching*, and often loses. Matter stands still; only the ownership graph rotates. (Sentimental goods, of course, are typed as unique — the catalogue has a category for "this particular piano," and no bookkeeping trick moves a grandmother's piano except a truck.)

### The touring band as a traveling salesman

A four-piece band wants to tour. Venues post offers — "one evening of live music, fitting within folk-adjacent, ninety minutes," priced on the venue's own scale, which its other offers anchor to door receipts — and the band posts its availability, plus a van with three cubic meters of spare cargo space. The solver that routes the tour is solving a classic route-optimization puzzle over the venue offers (which cities, what order, feasible drive times), and then it *monetizes the van*: every inter-city hop carries parcels from the general freight pool, and in two towns the amplifier haul is aggregated with a music shop's delivery run. A tour that made no sense as a sequence of fees becomes viable as a braid of loops. Musicians have always known the touring economy runs on favors, merch tables, and gear hauled in vans; this merely gives the folklore a type system and a settlement layer.

### The wedding compiled by a solver

A couple posts a budget and a concept: "wedding, fitting within celebration, eighty guests, this region, this date window." What comes back is not a quote but a compiled artifact. A farm barn, cleared and swept, whose owner never thought of it as an event space until the catalogue pointed out that "barn, cleared, two hundred square meters, weekend" fits within "event venue, rustic." Catering from a restaurant's dark Sunday — its kitchen and brigade idle by the quirk of a closing day. Chairs from a school's assembly hall, disaggregated Friday evening and re-aggregated by a return-transport leg before Monday's bell. Flowers from a grower whose payment leg loops through the photographer — who, it turns out, wanted a season of vegetables from that very grower. Weddings are famously overpriced because their coordination is bespoke, assembled once and thrown away; here the coordination *is* the product, manufactured by competing algorithms, every leg bonded, and the couple sees one price and one guarantee.

### Watts on wheels

Energy may be where loops first touch most households, because the offers are already being written by machines. A rooftop solar array produces its embarrassing noon surplus; the household posts it: "up to five kilowatt-hours, 11:00–15:00, at the meter." Three doors down, an electric car sits parked — as cars do, some ninety-five percent of their lives — and its owner posts the battery both ways: charging capacity at noon, discharge back to the grid at the evening peak. This is not futurism; bidirectional "vehicle-to-grid" programs are moving from pilots to products, with major carmakers now bundling the car, the two-way charger, and the tariff, and telling German customers to expect several hundred euros a year for lending the grid their parked battery. A school bus fleet — idle every July, batteries brimming — is a summer power plant that happens to have wheels. Even the freezer joins in: "demand flexibility, two kilowatt-hours deferrable, any two hours' notice" is an offer to pre-chill during the solar glut and coast through the peak, storing energy as cold food instead of charged lithium.

Watch what the loop machinery does with these parts. Energy stored in a battery is the *time-shifting* trick from the cider cellar — moving value from noon to seven p.m. Energy in a battery that then drives across town is transport through space and time at once: charge at the farm's solar carport at midday, park at the bakery at dawn, discharge into the ovens' first firing. The bakery pays in bread futures the café wanted; the café's leg closes back at the farm as the morning's pastries for the pickers. Kilowatt-hours, it turns out, are just very light cargo with a leaky container — and the catalogue prices the leak (storage loss, battery wear) like any other cost of carriage. Grid operators call the aggregate a virtual power plant; the loop economy calls it Tuesday.

### Bits are lighter than atoms

Offers need not concern matter at all. "Execution of one containerized computing job, up to eight gigabytes of memory, two hours, on hardware physically in the European Union" is an offer — the location clause is there because data-protection law is itself a category in the catalogue, and "hosted in the EU" is a thing a job can *require* and a datacenter can *satisfy*. "Transfer of five hundred gigabytes from cluster A to cluster B" is likewise an offer, and the catalogue is serenely indifferent to whether the leg is fulfilled by fiber or by a van full of hard drives — bandwidth economists have long joked, correctly, that a station wagon of tapes on the highway has magnificent throughput. A deep symmetry then surfaces on its own: *moving the computation to the data and moving the data to the computation are both just transport legs*, priced and solved like any other, so the placement question that cloud architects settle by hand and habit gets answered continuously, by competition.

And then the ouroboros. The decentralized storage network on which the offers themselves live — its storage commitments, its bandwidth, its postage-stamp-like prepayments — is made of exactly such digitally verifiable services. The marketplace's own substrate can be its first vertical: the earliest profitable loops can be loops that provision the very system that hosts them. This is more than a cute bootstrap; digital legs come with free, perfect oracles (the network can *prove* a chunk is stored), which makes them the ideal nursery for the guarantee machinery before it graduates to pianos.

### Disaster mode: the economy as immune system

A river floods. Within hours the offer book deforms around the event like spacetime around a mass. Transport into the valley reprices upward — not by decree but by arithmetic, because the nano-bet odds on "delivery into the flood zone succeeds" have collapsed, and guarantee costs flow straight into effective rates. Outbound, the book fills with urgency: evacuate my belongings, my animals, my server rack. Solvers — indifferent to sentiment, exquisitely sensitive to gradients — begin discovering loops no relief agency would have drafted. Evacuation vans that would return empty carry sandbags and pumps inbound. The wedding barn from three sections ago, suddenly idle, becomes bonded dry storage for a hundred households — *bonded* meaning its owner's deposit now stands behind a hundred small promises of dryness. A bridge agent wraps a hardware chain's pump inventory within minutes of the water rising. And the prediction-market layer becomes, as a pure side effect, the best disaster map in existence: the live odds on "delivery to this grid square succeeds" are a crowd-priced passability estimate, updated by people with money and vehicles on the line, faster than any official bulletin. The machinery that optimized cabbage triangles in peacetime becomes, without a line of new code, a logistics immune response. This is the deepest argument for the single uniform offer form: an economy with one data structure can redirect *all* of itself along any gradient, instantly.

### Care across distance

Time banks — I help your mother, someone helps mine — have existed for decades and always stall on the same reef: within one town and one service type, the web of offers is too sparse to close many circles. The loop economy is a time bank with transport, storage, and bridges welded on, which changes the geometry entirely. A daughter in São Paulo cannot cook for her father in Belo Horizonte. But she can tutor statistics over video for a nursing student in Rio, whose cousin drives the highway past Belo Horizonte every Friday, carrying meals cooked by a neighbor there — a neighbor whose own posted want, of all things, is help polishing a Wikidata entry, which the daughter can do in an evening. Four legs, three cities, one loop. And if the cousin prefers her leg paid partly in plain money — of course; nothing in the machinery cares whether value travels dressed as a favor, a voucher, or a banknote, and in a market this frictionless no domain can be fenced off from money-like things, nor needs to be. Money was never the obstacle to care at a distance; the missing match was. The daughter's tutoring always was worth a Friday delivery — there was simply no market granular enough to notice.

### Pollination futures and other ecological loops

Outlandish, as promised, yet every piece physically real: a beekeeper's hives are a service emitter — "pollination, radius three kilometers around these coordinates, April window." The orchards inside the radius are the obvious counterparties, but the loop can be richer: the orchard pays partly in blossom-honey rights (a disaggregation of its own product stream back to the beekeeper), a transport leg trucks the hives between staggered blooms — almond in late winter, apple in spring; the catalogue encodes flowering calendars as happily as timetables — and a nano-bet market on colony survival through the season becomes, as a side effect, a distributed pollinator-health monitor finer-grained than anything an environmental agency could fund. The same template extends wherever ecological services go unpaid for want of a match rather than want of value: grazing herds as fire-prevention ("goats, two weeks, this hillside" fits within "firebreak maintenance"), wetland acres as flood buffering sold to the downstream town, hedgerows as habitat sold to the subsidy program's verifier. Ecological services are systematically under-provided because they are systematically *unmatched*, and they are unmatched because they come in odd shapes. A marketplace whose entire premise is odd shapes is their natural habitat.

### Phantom capacity

Every scheduled system leaks capacity: the half-empty regional flight, the tour bus deadheading home, the refrigerated truck returning warm and empty, the restaurant dark on Mondays, the lecture hall on weekends, the electron microscope idle at 3 a.m., the dentist's cancellation at 2 p.m. Each is a phantom — real capacity with no market presence, invisible because listing it was never worth anyone's while. Bridge agents specializing in phantom-hunting will emerge: read the schedules, infer the slack, post the offers, take a spread. The cancellation becomes a discounted appointment offer with a two-hour validity window; the microscope's graveyard shift becomes a research service sold by the hour to a lab three time zones away. The endgame is an economy in which the difference between "a logistics company" and "a person with a van" is only a difference in offer volume — and in which the utilization of civilization's whole capital stock drifts quietly upward, because idleness has finally become legible.

## The solver ecology

The solvers deserve their own zoology, because an open competition will speciate.

**Motif miners** memorize recurring cycle shapes — the commuter–café–commuter relay, the empty-return-truck pairing, the dark-kitchen Sunday — and pattern-match fresh offers against the library: cheap, fast, first to the easy surplus. **Planners** treat loop construction the way classical artificial intelligence treats getting a robot across a kitchen: offers are actions with preconditions and effects on a world-state spread across space and time, and hierarchical decomposition ("deliver the parcel" becomes "get it to a hub, buffer it, get it from the hub") prunes the search the way navigation apps prune a continent's road network — by precomputing shortcuts through the hierarchy. The counterpart here is an index of offers organized by category, map cell, and time bucket, published as subscription feeds on the storage layer, so a solver interested in "refrigerated transport, this valley, this week" need never wade through the world's guitar lessons. **Embedding solvers** learn fuzzy numerical fingerprints of catalogue regions and propose *plausible* matches at high speed, which exact fits-within checking then verifies — intuition followed by proof, and cheap intuition is worth a great deal when the space is astronomical. **Speculators** are the strangest and most consequential species: a solver that finds an *almost*-loop, one leg short of closing, can post the missing leg itself — buy the flour from a bridge, hire the courier, underwrite the gap — becoming a market maker of last resort and, in aggregate, the force that fills the structural holes in the graph. When solvers begin posting offers designed to bait *other* solvers' loops into routing through them, the ecosystem will have invented its own high finance, for better and worse.

For worse is a live possibility, so competition needs referee rules. Continuous, first-come settlement invites sniping: a fast solver watches a rival's pending loop and lodges a copy a millisecond earlier — the very arms race that had financiers spending fortunes to straighten a cable between two cities, until market designers showed the cure: run time in discrete beats. Settlement therefore proceeds in periodic batch auctions — every few seconds or minutes, all pending proposals compete, and the winner is the set of loops delivering the greatest surplus *to participants*, most of it rebated to them. Speed stops mattering inside a beat; the contest collapses onto quality of solution, which is where we want the evolutionary pressure. Solvers post bonds against submitting infeasible loops, accumulate reputation, and earn their sliver. Their infrastructure — the indices, the feasibility checkers, the catalogue reasoners — is where most of the engineering in the whole system will actually live, and rightly so: the protocol's one sacred duty is to keep *verification* of a proposed loop cheap and neutral, while *discovery* stays someone else's expensive, competitive problem.

One more honesty, borrowed from complexity science — the study of systems whose global behavior emerges from the interactions of many parts, anthills and weather fronts and, canonically, economies. Its hardest-won lesson is humility: such systems are not predicted, only cultivated. "More is different," as the physicist Philip Anderson put it; the aggregate invents behaviors its parts know nothing of. Nobody forecast that an open solver competition on a token exchange would drive out the incumbent aggregators within two years, and nobody will forecast the solver species of the loop economy either — the strategies sketched above are the *obvious* ones, which is precisely why they are incomplete. This is not a flaw to be engineered away but a property to be governed for: keep the rules few and legible, keep verification cheap, keep entry open, and let the ecology do what ecologies do — surprise you, mostly from directions you were not looking.

## Judges without swords

Between commitment and performance stands the question every skeptic reaches first: *who says the cabbages actually arrived?*

The contract cannot see cabbages. It sees only witnesses it has agreed in advance to believe — **oracles**, in the trade's jargon. An oracle can be as thin as the counterparty's countersignature ("received, in good order"), as mechanical as the parcel locker reporting a deposit at 17:42, as vivid as a timestamped photograph, or, for digital legs, as absolute as a cryptographic proof that the data really is stored. Each offer names its oracle in advance, and the oracle's cost is part of the leg's price; cheap legs get cheap witnesses, pianos get photographers.

But witnesses disagree, photographs mislead, and cellos fail to fit crates. So above the oracles sits a second, older institution, rebuilt: the **arbitrator** — a private judge who issues rulings and nothing else. No bailiffs, no warrants; none are needed, because every promise in the system already has money locked behind it. A ruling is a signature that releases or slashes a deposit, and so it enforces itself. The loser's further punishment is not prison but *price*: a repriced reputation, wider guarantee premia on their next hundred offers, nano-bet odds that tilt against them. Enforcement, in other words, is economic gravity rather than physical force.

This sounds novel and is nearly medieval. The merchants of Europe's great fairs ran their own courts — the Law Merchant — whose judgments no king enforced; a trader who ignored a ruling simply found no counterparties at the next fair, and that was enough. The radical wing of economics has long argued the pattern generalizes — David Friedman's *The Machinery of Freedom* sketched a whole legal order of competing, private rights-enforcement agencies — and the internet age has begun testing gentler versions: decentralized juries already arbitrate online disputes by staked vote, and every large marketplace's resolution center is a private court in embarrassed disguise. The loop economy makes the pattern explicit and, crucially, *makes adjudication itself an offer*: "arbitration: disputes over bicycle repair, this region, fee twenty, ruling within seventy-two hours" sits in the same book as the bicycle repairs it will someday judge. Offers name their arbitrator in advance the way contracts name a jurisdiction; arbitrators compete on speed, fee, and the market's trust in their fairness — measurable, morbidly enough, by the nano-bet odds on their own rulings being overturned on appeal. Appeals ascend a ladder agreed in advance: the cheap fast judge first, a slower panel above, and for the rare catastrophic case, the ordinary courts of the territory remain — the system is an overlay on civilization, not a secession from it.

## The guarantee fabric

Now the plies of the safety weave can be seen together. At bottom, the bonds: every promise collateralized, every deposit released by verified performance or moved by a ruling. Above them, the nano-bets — but note the crucial aggregation. One thin betting market per parcel would be illiquid theater; nobody prices "will Mrs. Ilic's Tuesday jar of honey arrive." The markets that matter are pooled: *per-issuer* default rates, *per-route* reliability, *per-oracle* honesty, *per-arbitrator* reversal rates. Their odds flow into every leg's effective price as a risk premium, continuously, so that a courier's sloppy month costs them business by Thursday. Professional **guarantors** then arise — underwriters who stand behind other people's legs for a fee, quoting asymmetric terms (tiny stakes against high-probability performance, large payouts on rare default) and netting their exposure across the graph: five hundred Tuesday parcels are a portfolio, not a sum, and capital requirements respect the correlations. Insurance, like adjudication, is just another offer.

Then the braid: backup legs pre-committed at fractional bond, triggered automatically by oracle failure or by odds breaching a threshold. And beneath everything, the quietest ply: the catalogue's own statements are bonded speech. "Object class X fits container class Y" is an assertion someone staked money on; when a loop fails because the cello did not, in fact, fit the crate, the dispute resolves against whoever staked the false claim, with stakes scaled to how load-bearing the statement is — a claim a million loops route through must post more than a claim about vintage doorknobs. Human knowledge bases have always had a fatal luxury: being wrong is free. Here, for the first time, an ontology has an error bar denominated in money. Offers pin the exact catalogue version they were written against — content-addressing makes this free — so no one can move the semantic ground under a committed loop.

## Trading without being watched

There is a reading of everything above that should chill you: a public book of everyone's offers — what they own, what they need, where they will be, and when — is the most complete surveillance instrument ever proposed. Coarsening helps (publish the map cell, not the address; the week, not the hour) and staged disclosure helps more (precise coordinates revealed only to the matched counterparty, after commitment). But an adversary correlating coarse offers over months can still sketch a life. If the loop economy is to deserve existence, privacy cannot be a settings page; it has to be load-bearing architecture. Fortunately the cryptography of the last decade seems almost purpose-built for it.

Start with sealed envelopes. Content-addressing already means an offer can be published as a *commitment* — the fingerprint now, the contents later — so presence in the market needn't mean disclosure to the market. The heavier lifting falls to **zero-knowledge proofs**: protocols by which one party convinces another that a statement is true while revealing nothing else — the canonical party trick is proving you are over eighteen without showing your birthdate. Transposed here: the sender proves *the parcel fits within the courier's declared box* without revealing its dimensions; proves *the pickup lies inside the courier's corridor* without revealing the address; proves *my ratio clears your ratio* without revealing either number, which is a sealed-bid auction in cryptographic dress. The solver, remarkably, can be told enough to find the loop without being told what anyone is trading: matching can run on blinded categories — techniques like private set intersection let two lists discover their overlap and nothing else — and the more exotic tool of homomorphic encryption (arithmetic performed directly on sealed data, the computer producing an answer to a question it never saw) is, honestly, still too costly for wholesale matching, but entirely plausible for the narrow final checks, and getting cheaper every year. Settlement has its own rails: the liquid legs can run through privacy-preserving currencies — Monero, Zcash, Ether behind privacy layers — and settlement legs can land at fresh, unlinkable addresses per loop, so that even the settlement graph paints no portrait. At the posting layer, offers can travel through mixing relays and be published by delegated brokers, and decoy offers — real enough to carry small bonds, so flooding costs money — give traffic analysis a fog to squint through.

None of this is free. Privacy trades against matchability: the coarser and more sealed the offer, the fewer solvers can route through it, and each offer chooses its own point on that dial. Which means — a pleasing inversion — the market will *price privacy itself*, and for the first time we may learn what confidentiality is actually worth per kilogram-kilometer. Some legs will pay a premium to stay dark; some counterparties will pay a premium to deal only with those who do.

## The dark side, honestly

Every gallery needs its chamber of horrors, and gradients attract predators as reliably as they attract solvers. **Wash loops** — cycles spun among colluding identities to farm surplus rebates or inflate reputations — must be made unprofitable by fee design and bond friction rather than by identity policing, or the system decays into passport-checking. The optimizer itself has a moral hazard: left naive, it routes preferentially through the *least* reliable makers, whose offers trade at the widest, juiciest ratios precisely because they are risky — so the risk model must live inside the objective function, discounting surplus by braid-adjusted failure probability, or "most profitable loop" quietly degenerates into "loop most likely to explode." The catalogue is a political surface: whoever can cheaply insert fits-within claims steers all matching, which is why assertion bonds must scale with an edge's centrality, and why catalogue governance deserves the paranoia usually reserved for central banks. Dominant bridges are chokepoints in waiting — the wrapper everyone's loops close through is a platform monopoly in embryo, so bridge plurality is not a nicety but a constitutional requirement. And the speculator-solvers who so usefully fill structural holes are the same creatures who will someday corner a thin market in cellar space the week before harvest. None of these is a reason not to build; each is a reason to assume the adversary is already reading the whitepaper.

## The path in

Strip the fantasy away and the construction order almost writes itself. Begin where oracles are free: the storage network's own commitments, provable in mathematics, are the nursery — the marketplace's first vertical is its own substrate, loops that provision the system that hosts them. Extend to digital services generally, where performance is a checksum. Then the first physical niches, chosen for low stakes and high density — campus parcel relays, neighborhood grocery legs through locker banks — where photographic and countersignature oracles suffice and the guarantee fabric can accumulate its actuarial tables on stakes no one cries over. Bridges throughout, because density is oxygen. Let the arbitrators, the guarantors, the phantom-hunters, and the privacy rails harden on ten thousand small disputes. And only then ship a piano.

The deepest bet, though, was never technical. It is that an enormous fraction of possible economic life simply fails to occur — not because it wouldn't be mutually profitable, but because the cost of *discovering* it exceeds the surplus it would yield. The empty van beside the waiting parcel. The dark kitchen beside the hungry wedding. The idle microscope beside the stalled thesis. The noon sunlight beside the evening peak. The lonely skill beside the distant need. Economists have a name for matter that must exist because its gravity bends everything, though no telescope resolves it. The loop economy is a machine for making the economy's dark matter visible — one closed loop, one small bonded promise, one harvested coincidence at a time.
